Overview

Background

Effective malaria control depends on the timely, accurate linkage of surveillance data (suspected fever, confirmed malaria and treatment of confirmed malaria cases) with commodity consumption data (RDTs used and ACTs dispensed), and visibility of stock levels at facility and district level. This workshop brings together surveillance, case management, and supply-chain staff to align these indicators, build minimum/maximum stock-level and reorder logic into routine reporting, and configure epidemic threshold alerts within eIDSR.

Overall objectives

  1. Strengthen the linkage between suspected fever cases tested and RDTs consumed, and between confirmed malaria cases and ACTs dispensed, within routine facility and district reporting.
  2. Build participant capacity to compute Average Monthly Consumption (AMC), Minimum Stock Level, Maximum Stock Level, Reorder Level and Safety Stock for RDTs and ACTs.
  3. Configure and/or reinforce use of the eIDSR platform to capture, validate, and visualize malaria commodity and case-based indicators in real time.
  4. Enable automated/semi-automated stock alerts within eIDSR to trigger timely replenishment and mitigate stockouts.
  5. Build capacity to define, set, and interpret malaria epidemic thresholds (alert and epidemic/action thresholds) using historical facility/district data.
  6. Produce facility- and district-level action plans, including SOPs for threshold breaches and stock alerts.

Target participants

  • National Surveillance Officers
  • Malaria Epidemiology Surveillance Monitoring and Evaluation Officers
  • Pharmacists/Stores/Logistics Officers
  • IT/Data Scientist Specialists
  • eIDSR data managers

The five days

Key indicators covered

Testing Rate(Number of RDTs used ÷ Number of suspected fever cases seen) × 100
Test Positivity Rate(Confirmed malaria cases ÷ Number tested) × 100
ACT Utilization Ratio(Number of ACTs dispensed ÷ Number of confirmed malaria cases) × 100
Stock measuresStock on Hand, Average Monthly Consumption (AMC), Minimum Stock Level, Maximum Stock Level, Reorder Level, Months of Stock (MOS)
ThresholdsAlert Threshold and Epidemic/Action Threshold for malaria case trends

Presentation outline

03

Background

1

Malaria transmission in Sierra Leone is perennial, accounting for 40% of clinical consultations.

2

Health workers diagnose suspected malaria cases using rapid diagnostic tests (RDT) and microscopy, with facility-level results reported to the Health Management Information System (HMIS) as monthly aggregates.

3

Antimalarial commodity stocks (RDTS, ACTs, ITNs, SP etc) are reported to the Logistics Management Information System (LMIS).

4

NMCP needs to use eIDSR weekly malaria data to track malaria commodities because weekly surveillance is the earliest, most sensitive signal of commodity pressure.

5

DHIS2 monthly LMIS data alone is too slow to prevent stockouts in high-burden or hard-to-reach districts/chiefdoms.

04

Weekly eIDSR data detects sudden increases in malaria cases

Monthly LMIS reporting shows consumption after 30 days, but malaria case increases happen within days. Weekly eIDSR data allows NMCP to see:

  • Sharp increases in suspected malaria
  • Rising test positivity
  • Increased confirmed cases
  • Increased ACT consumption pressure
This early warning helps NMCP anticipate where RDTs and ACTs will run out before the monthly LMIS report arrives.
05

Malaria case trends to predict commodity consumption

RDT and ACT consumption are directly linked to:

  • Number of suspected cases (RDT use)
  • Number of confirmed cases (ACT use)

When eIDSR shows a spike in cases:

  • RDT use will increase immediately
  • ACT use will increase within 1 to 3 days
Weekly data allows NMCP to forecast consumption and trigger emergency resupply.
06

Preventing stockouts especially in hard-to-reach areas

Districts like Bonthe, Moyamba, Port Loko, Kambia and riverine PHUs face:

  • Boat-dependent transport
  • Delayed deliveries
  • Limited buffer stock capacity

Monthly LMIS data is too slow for these areas. Weekly eIDSR data enables:

  • Early identification of PHUs at risk
  • Faster mobilization of emergency stocks
  • Better planning of boat logistics
07

eIDSR data to validate LMIS consumption

LMIS sometimes shows:

  • Under-reported consumption
  • Over-reported closing balances
  • Missing reports
  • Delayed submissions

Weekly eIDSR data helps NMCP detect inconsistencies:

High cases + low RDT use→under-reporting
Low cases + high ACT use→irrational use
High cases + zero ACT use→stockout
This improves data quality and supply chain accuracy.
08

Weekly surveillance supports outbreak detection

Malaria outbreaks begin with:

  • eIDSR detects these Rising fever cases
  • Rising test positivity
  • Rising confirmed malaria cases

eIDSR detects patterns weeks earlier than DHIS2 LMIS. Early detection allows NMCP to:

  • Pre-position RDTs and ACTs
  • Activate Rapid Response Teams
  • Prevent facility-level stockouts during outbreaks
09

Weekly surveillance for rapid commodity response

NMCP can use weekly eIDSR trends to:

  • Adjust buffer stock levels
  • Prioritize high-burden PHUs
  • Plan distribution routes
  • Allocate commodities based on real-time burden
This is essential for districts with seasonal spikes (e.g., rainy season peaks).
10

eIDSR data for accountability and targeted supervision

Supervisors can use weekly trends to:

  • Identify PHUs with unusual patterns
  • Target facilities for supervision
  • Verify stock cards and registers
  • Ensure correct RDT/ACT use
This strengthens both surveillance and supply chain governance.

Epidemic Threshold Guide

Guide

A practical step‑by‑step guide to develop malaria epidemic thresholds

Grounded in standard surveillance guidance

Overview

Developing epidemic thresholds for malaria is about turning routine surveillance data into an early‑warning trigger that is statistically sound, operationally practical, and context‑specific, especially in endemic, highly seasonal settings like Sierra Leone.

1Clarify purpose and scope2Select indicators3Assemble and prepare historical data4Account for seasonality and stratification5Choose threshold method6Compute thresholds for each unit7Validate against historical events8Define operational rules9Integrate into routine surveillance systems10Review and update thresholds regularly
Step 1

Clarify purpose and scope

  • Define objective: Are you aiming to detect outbreaks early, monitor seasonal peaks, or compare districts?
  • Choose geographic unit: District, chiefdom, or facility catchment: Decide on the level at which decisions are made.
  • Choose time unit: For malaria, weekly thresholds are standard for epidemic detection; monthly can be used for broader trend monitoring.
Step 2

Select indicators

  • Core indicators (from routine data):
    • Confirmed malaria cases: by RDT/microscopy, disaggregated by age (<5, ≥5) if possible.
    • Test positivity rate (TPR): positive tests ÷ total tests.
    • Malaria admissions or severe malaria cases: for severe epidemic signals.
  • Primary epidemic indicator: For endemic countries, weekly confirmed malaria cases per facility is usually the main threshold variable.
  • Test Positivity Rate is used as a supporting indicator.
Step 3

Assemble and prepare historical data

  • Data period: Aim for 3–5 years of good‑quality weekly data, including at least 2–3 transmission seasons.
  • Data sources: DHIS2, paper registers, NMCP weekly bulletins, lab registers, inpatient records.
  • Data cleaning:
    • Check completeness: identify weeks with missing or doubtful values.
    • Correct obvious errors: extreme outliers due to data entry mistakes.
    • Flag anomalies: weeks with known stockouts or reporting changes.
  • Adjust for population and service changes:
    • Population: use cases per 1,000 population if catchment populations differ.
    • Service access: note major changes (new facilities, mass campaigns) that may alter case detection.
Step 4

Account for seasonality and stratification

  • Seasonal pattern: In Sierra Leone, malaria is strongly linked to rainfall, thresholds must reflect expected seasonal peaks rather than a flat annual average.
  • Stratify where needed:
    • By transmission intensity: high vs. moderate districts.
    • By age group: under‑5 vs. ≥5 if program actions differ.
    • By facility type: hospitals vs. PHUs.
  • Create “expected” weekly baseline: For each week (Week 1–52), compute the typical level of cases using historical data from the same week in previous years.
Step 5

Choose threshold method

Use one or more of these standard approaches:

5.1

Simple mean + standard deviation (SD)

For each week w:

  • Compute the mean of cases over baseline years:
μw = 1n∑i=1n Cw,i
  • Compute the standard deviation SDw.
  • Set epidemic threshold as:
Tw = μw + 2 × SDw
  • Interpretation: Weekly cases above Tw suggest an unusual increase, potential epidemic.
5.2

Percentile‑based thresholds

For each week w:

  • Order historical weekly counts for that week.
  • Use the 75th or 90th percentile as threshold.
  • Advantage: Less sensitive to extreme outliers than mean+2SD.
5.3

Cumulative sum (C‑SUM) or moving average

  • Moving average: Smooth weekly data using a 3‑week moving average to reduce noise, then apply mean+2SD or percentile on smoothed values.
  • C‑SUM: Track cumulative deviation from expected values; when cumulative excess exceeds a set limit, flag an epidemic. (Useful where weekly data are noisy but trends matter.)
Step 6

Compute thresholds for each unit

  • Per district/facility:
    • For each week (1–52), calculate μw, SDw, and threshold Tw (or percentile).
    • Store results in a threshold table: rows = weeks, columns = districts/facilities.
  • Visualize:
    • Plot historical weekly cases with the threshold line overlaid.
    • Check that thresholds sit above normal seasonal peaks but still detect known past epidemics.
Step 7

Validate against historical events

  • Identify known epidemics: Use past reports, bulletins, or field knowledge to list weeks/periods with confirmed outbreaks.
  • Test performance:
    • Sensitivity: Did thresholds flag these known epidemics?
    • Specificity: Did thresholds avoid frequent false alarms in normal years?
  • Refine parameters:
    • Adjust from mean+2SD to mean+1.5SD or change percentile (e.g., 75th→80th) if thresholds are too lax or too strict.
    • Consider separate thresholds for high‑transmission vs. moderate‑transmission districts.
Step 8

Define operational rules

  • Trigger definition:
    • Single‑week rule: epidemic alert if cases > threshold in two consecutive weeks.
    • Multi‑indicator rule: alert if cases exceed threshold and TPR rises above a set level (e.g., >50%).
  • Response package: For each alert, pre‑define actions:
    • Verification: rapid data review, confirm no reporting error.
    • Field investigation: assess local context, vector situation, stock levels.
    • Interventions: targeted IRS, LLIN top‑ups, community case management intensification, communication campaigns.
  • Documentation: Include thresholds and response rules in national SOPs and surveillance guidelines, with clear roles for NMCP, districts, and facilities.
Step 9

Integrate into routine surveillance systems

  • DHIS2 configuration:
    • Build weekly malaria dashboards showing observed vs. threshold for each district/facility.
    • Set automatic flags (color‑coding) when thresholds are exceeded.
  • Weekly bulletin:
    • Include a section on “districts above epidemic threshold” with brief interpretation.
    • Track alerts and responses over time.
  • Capacity building: Train district surveillance officers and NMCP staff on:
    • How thresholds are calculated.
    • How to interpret alerts.
    • How to document and escalate responses.
Step 10

Review and update thresholds regularly

  • Annual review: Recalculate thresholds every 1–2 years as more data become available or transmission patterns change.
  • Adjust for major interventions: After mass LLIN campaigns, IRS scale‑up, or introduction of new tools (e.g., malaria vaccine), baseline levels may drop; thresholds must be recalibrated.
  • Monitor performance: Track how often alerts occur, how often they correspond to true outbreaks, and whether response actions are timely and effective.
